# NASA Space Apps Challenge
# Research Guide: Astronaut Healthcare Monitoring System

**Purpose:** Identify useful NASA resources, understand relevant astronaut health risks, and translate available research data into a practical healthcare-monitoring prototype.

---

## 1. Why NASA's Open Science Data Repository (OSDR) Matters

NASA's **Open Science Data Repository (OSDR)** is one of the most valuable starting points for this challenge. It provides access to spaceflight biological and health-related research datasets.

Use it to:

- Investigate physiological and biological changes associated with spaceflight.
- Explore research into radiation, microgravity, immune response, and other health risks.
- Find datasets that can inform system requirements and analytical methods.
- Support your prototype with real research data where suitable data is publicly available.

**Important:** OSDR is primarily a research-data resource. It should not be assumed to provide live astronaut medical telemetry. Research datasets may be collected before, during, or after a mission, and some raw data may have access restrictions.

**Main portal:** https://osdr.nasa.gov/bio/repo/

## 2. Useful NASA Resources and Applications

### 2.1 OSDR Data Repository

**Priority: Very High**

Use the repository to discover space biology and astronaut-related datasets.

Potential uses:

- Study physiological changes associated with spaceflight.
- Investigate health risks and biological responses.
- Find research data for exploratory analysis and prototype testing.

Link: https://osdr.nasa.gov/bio/repo/

### 2.2 RadLab

**Priority: High for Radiation Monitoring**

RadLab is an OSDR visualization resource for exploring radiation-related measurements and data.

Potential uses:

- Understand radiation exposure in space.
- Explore dose and dose-rate measurements where available.
- Inform radiation-exposure indicators and risk visualizations.

**Prototype opportunity:** Build a radiation exposure panel that displays available measurements, cumulative exposure estimates, trends, and configurable warning thresholds.

Make the units, assumptions, and data source visible.

Find RadLab through the OSDR tools and applications area.

### 2.3 Environmental Data Application (EDA)

**Priority: High for Environmental Monitoring**

Use the Environmental Data Application to explore International Space Station environmental and hardware telemetry.

Potentially relevant measurements include:

- Temperature
- Humidity
- Oxygen
- Carbon dioxide
- Radiation or other environmental measurements, depending on the dataset

Potential uses:

- Monitor the environment surrounding the crew.
- Detect unusual environmental trends.
- Connect environmental conditions with health-monitoring context.

**Prototype opportunity:** Display environmental measurements over time and flag values that cross clearly documented thresholds.

Do not invent thresholds; use authoritative source documentation.

Find EDA through the OSDR tools and applications area.

### 2.4 Inspiration4 Human Research Data

**Priority: High for Human Biological Research**

The Inspiration4 mission generated human research data from four commercial astronauts, with samples collected around the mission.

Potential research areas include:

- Immune-system changes.
- Biological responses to spaceflight.
- Differences in individual responses.
- Before-, during-, and after-flight comparisons where data is available.

**Prototype opportunity:** Explore how biological indicators vary across mission phases. Use this research to explain why health monitoring should consider an astronaut's baseline and individual differences.

**Limitation:** Biological sample data is not the same as continuous vital-sign telemetry. Do not present it as live heart-rate, blood-pressure, or other real-time sensor data.

Find Inspiration4 studies through the OSDR repository search.

### 2.5 GeneLab Visualization Tools

**Priority: Useful for Deeper Biological Analysis**

GeneLab-related visualization tools can help explore biological datasets.

Possible visualizations include:

- Heatmaps
- Principal component analysis (PCA) plots
- Gene-expression comparisons
- Pair plots
- Volcano plots

Potential uses:

- Explore biological changes associated with spaceflight.
- Compare samples or experimental conditions.
- Identify patterns that may deserve further scientific investigation.

**Limitation:** These are research-analysis tools. A pattern in gene-expression data is not automatically a clinically meaningful health alert.

Find relevant tools and datasets through OSDR.

## 3. Astronaut Health Risks to Investigate

The following table connects broad health-risk areas with potentially relevant research sources and possible software capabilities.

| Health-risk area | Potentially relevant resource/data | Possible system capability |
|---|---|---|
| Radiation exposure | RadLab and radiation-related OSDR studies | Display exposure measurements, trends, and cumulative-dose estimates where supported |
| Immune-system changes | Inspiration4 and GeneLab human studies | Show research-informed biological trends and individual variation |
| Cardiovascular changes | Human physiological and spaceflight studies | Monitor available cardiovascular measurements and detect deviations from personal baselines |
| Bone and muscle loss | OSDR spaceflight biological studies | Track relevant measurements across time and mission phases |
| Environmental hazards | Environmental Data Application (EDA) | Monitor available environmental telemetry such as CO₂, oxygen, temperature, and humidity |
| Sleep and behavioral changes | OSDR behavioral and human research studies | Track sleep or behavioral indicators when suitable data is available |

### Important Distinctions

- **Environmental telemetry** describes conditions in the spacecraft or habitat.
- **Physiological telemetry** describes measurements from the human body, such as heart rate.
- **Biological research data** may include molecular or cellular measurements from collected samples.
- **Behavioral data** may describe sleep, cognition, workload, or other human-performance factors.

These data types are not interchangeable.

A strong system should identify the source, measurement unit, timestamp, collection method, and limitations of every data stream.

## 4. Suggested System Architecture

A practical high-level architecture can be organized into three layers.

```mermaid
flowchart TB
    A["Layer 1: Data Collection"]
    A1["Physiological Sensors"]
    A2["Environmental Telemetry"]
    A3["Crew Input"]

    A --> A1
    A --> A2
    A --> A3

    B["Layer 2: Health Intelligence"]
    B1["Data Validation and Quality Checks"]
    B2["Personal Baseline and Trend Analysis"]
    B3["Anomaly Detection"]
    B4["Risk Assessment"]

    A1 --> B
    A2 --> B
    A3 --> B

    B --> B1
    B1 --> B2
    B2 --> B3
    B3 --> B4

    C["Layer 3: Decision Support"]
    C1["Health Alerts"]
    C2["Risk Prioritization"]
    C3["Crew-facing Recommendations"]
    C4["Ground Communication"]

    B4 --> C
    C --> C1
    C --> C2
    C --> C3
    C --> C4
```

### Layer 1 — Data Collection

Collect and organize:

- Physiological sensor measurements, if available.
- Environmental telemetry.
- Crew-reported symptoms or wellness information.
- Research datasets for offline analysis and validation.

For each data point, preserve its timestamp, unit, source, and quality status.

### Layer 2 — Health Intelligence

Analyze the incoming information:

- Validate missing, delayed, or implausible measurements.
- Establish individual baselines where appropriate.
- Analyze trends over time.
- Detect unusual values or changes.
- Assess risks using documented rules and explainable methods.

Avoid treating one unusual reading as proof of a medical problem. Account for sensor quality, context, and repeated measurements.

### Layer 3 — Decision Support

Present useful information to the crew and mission support team:

- Clear health alerts with severity and explanation.
- Prioritized risks.
- Trends and relevant historical context.
- Suggested next steps based on approved protocols.
- Communication and escalation status.

The system should support human decision-making rather than claim to replace qualified medical judgment.

## 5. How to Search the OSDR Repository

When searching the repository, try these terms:

| Search term | Why it is useful |
|---|---|
| `Human` | Find research involving human participants |
| `Spaceflight` | Find experiments related to space missions |
| `Radiation` | Find radiation-exposure research |
| `Microgravity` | Find studies of reduced-gravity effects |
| `Immune` | Find immune-system research |
| `Cardiovascular` | Find heart and circulation research |
| `Behavior` | Find behavioral and human-performance research |

### Suggested Search Workflow

1. Open the [OSDR repository](https://osdr.nasa.gov/bio/repo/).
2. Search for a health-risk topic, such as `Immune` or `Cardiovascular`.
3. Prioritize human studies when the project concerns astronaut health.
4. Check whether the study relates to spaceflight.
5. Read the study description and data-use documentation.
6. Inspect the available variables, units, sample sizes, and collection time points.
7. Check whether the data is publicly downloadable or access-restricted.
8. Record the dataset identifier and cite the study in your project.

### Dataset Evaluation Checklist

- [ ] What research question was the dataset created to investigate?
- [ ] Who or what was studied?
- [ ] Was the study conducted in spaceflight, on Earth, or in a simulation?
- [ ] What variables are included?
- [ ] What are the measurement units?
- [ ] When and how often were measurements collected?
- [ ] Is the dataset raw, processed, or summarized?
- [ ] Are there missing values or known quality limitations?
- [ ] Is the dataset publicly accessible?
- [ ] What conclusions can and cannot be drawn from it?

## 6. Recommended Starting Combination

Rather than trying to use every dataset, begin with three complementary perspectives.

### A. Inspiration4 — What Happens Inside the Astronaut?

Use human biological research to understand changes associated with spaceflight and the importance of individual variation.

### B. RadLab — What Exposure Does the Astronaut Experience?

Use radiation-related information to understand exposure measurements and how to communicate trends and cumulative exposure.

### C. EDA — What Is Happening Around the Astronaut?

Use environmental telemetry to understand habitat conditions and identify potentially hazardous environmental trends.

Together, these resources can help the team investigate the relationship between:

1. The astronaut's environment.
2. The astronaut's exposure to spaceflight hazards.
3. The astronaut's biological or physiological response.

This is a useful foundation for an integrated monitoring concept.

## 7. Practical Prototype Development Plan

### Phase 1 — Research

- Select one primary health-risk area.
- Find two or three relevant NASA studies or datasets.
- Document variables, units, collection methods, and limitations.
- Identify which measurements are real research data and which would need to be simulated.

### Phase 2 — Data Preparation

- Download accessible datasets.
- Inspect their structure and quality.
- Normalize timestamps and units where appropriate.
- Handle missing data explicitly.
- Keep source information attached to every measurement.

### Phase 3 — Monitoring Interface

Build a dashboard with:

- Current or sample measurements.
- Historical trends.
- Personal baseline comparisons where justified.
- Data-quality indicators.
- Alert history.
- Clear source and timestamp information.

### Phase 4 — Intelligence

Start with explainable rules and statistical methods before adding complex AI:

- Threshold-based alerts using documented limits.
- Trend detection.
- Baseline deviation.
- Missing or stale data detection.
- Risk prioritization with explanations.

### Phase 5 — Demonstration

If real-time astronaut telemetry is unavailable:

- Use NASA research data for evidence and scientific context.
- Use clearly labeled simulated sensor streams for the live demonstration.
- Explain which parts are real, simulated, or proposed.
- Demonstrate how the system handles missing data, abnormal readings, and alert escalation.

## 8. What Could Make the Project Stand Out?

The strongest concept is not simply another dashboard displaying vital signs.

A more valuable direction is an **integrated astronaut health decision-support system** that connects:

- Environmental conditions.
- Exposure history.
- Physiological measurements.
- Individual baselines.
- Changes over time.
- Explainable alerts and recommended actions.

### Example Scenario

If environmental CO₂ measurements rise while an astronaut reports discomfort, the system can:

1. Bring those signals together.
2. Show their timestamps and data quality.
3. Highlight the relevant environmental trend.
4. Notify the appropriate crew or ground-support personnel according to the mission's approved procedures.

This example illustrates contextual monitoring. It is not a validated medical diagnosis or proof that CO₂ caused a symptom.

### Potential Differentiators

**1. Personalized Baselines**

Compare measurements with an astronaut's own historical patterns rather than relying only on population averages.

**2. Multisource Monitoring**

Combine environmental, physiological, and crew-reported information.

**3. Explainable Alerts**

Show what changed, which measurements support the alert, and what information is missing.

**4. Data-quality Awareness**

Identify stale, missing, or unreliable measurements before generating conclusions.

**5. Offline Resilience**

Design for communication delays and temporary disconnection, with local data storage and clear synchronization status.

**6. Human-centered Escalation**

Make alerts understandable and route them through appropriate human-approved procedures.

## 9. Safety, Scientific Validity, and Limitations

Keep these principles visible throughout development:

- Do not claim that the prototype diagnoses diseases.
- Do not claim that an unvalidated model predicts medical emergencies.
- Do not invent clinical thresholds or radiation limits.
- Distinguish actual NASA data from simulated demonstration data.
- Preserve units, timestamps, and dataset provenance.
- Explain uncertainty and missing data.
- Avoid interpreting molecular research findings as direct clinical measurements.
- Treat alerts as decision support requiring appropriate human review.
- Use authoritative NASA and medical sources for any health-related thresholds or procedures.
