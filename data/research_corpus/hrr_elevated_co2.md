# Risk of Adverse Health Effects from Elevated Carbon Dioxide in Spaceflight

## Operational Context and Spaceflight Exposure
In terrestrial environments, standard carbon dioxide levels remain near 0.04% (0.3 mmHg). Aboard the International Space Station (ISS), closed-loop Environmental Control and Life Support Systems (ECLSS) maintain cabin CO2 between 2.0 and 4.0 mmHg (0.26% to 0.53%). Operational excursions frequently reach 4.5 to 6.0 mmHg during crew sleep periods, exercise sessions, or Carbon Dioxide Removal Assembly (CDRA) maintenance cycles.

## Headache and Physiological Susceptibility
Epidemiological analyses across ISS Expeditions indicate an increased risk of crew-reported headaches at cabin CO2 partial pressures above 4.0 mmHg. When CO2 partial pressure exceeds 4.5 mmHg (approximately 5.9 kPa), the predicted probability of an astronaut experiencing headache doubles compared to nominal 2.5 mmHg baselines. Microgravity induces cephalad fluid shifts that may heighten intracranial venous congestion, causing cerebral vasodilation and reducing tolerance to hypercapnic exposure.

## Non-Causal Decision Support Guidelines
Crew medical officers and flight surgeons must recognize that temporal correlation between CO2 spikes and reported cephalic discomfort does not establish single-variable clinical causation. Individual astronaut sensitivity variations, sleep fragmentation, and physical exertion are confounding factors.
