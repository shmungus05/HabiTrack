# Relevant Research Gaps for an Astronaut Healthcare Monitoring System

**Project:** NASA Space Apps Challenge — Healthcare Monitoring Software for Astronauts

**Purpose:** Identify unresolved research challenges and engineering gaps that a software-based astronaut healthcare monitoring system could address.

---

# 1. Executive Summary

Astronaut healthcare monitoring is more complex than collecting vital signs and displaying them on a dashboard.

Spaceflight changes the human body, creates unusual environmental exposures, and makes access to medical expertise and treatment difficult, particularly during deep-space missions.

A useful healthcare monitoring system should answer four questions:

1. **What is changing?** Detect deviations from an astronaut's personal health baseline.
2. **Why might it be changing?** Correlate physiological, behavioral, and environmental signals.
3. **How urgent is it?** Communicate risk and uncertainty.
4. **What should happen next?** Support astronauts and medical teams with evidence-based decision support.

The opportunities in this document are a combination of scientific research gaps, operational challenges, and software engineering opportunities. Not every item represents a completely unsolved NASA research problem.

# 2. Major Research Gaps

## Gap 1: Personalized Health Baselines and Early Detection

### Problem

A universal medical threshold may not adequately represent every astronaut.

Individual astronauts have different physiological characteristics, and their normal measurements may change during spaceflight.

A measurement that appears normal according to a general threshold may represent an unusual change for a particular astronaut.

### Why It Matters

- Health deterioration may develop gradually.
- Fixed thresholds may fail to identify early changes.
- Different astronauts respond differently to microgravity.
- Individual health patterns may change throughout a mission.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a personalized health monitoring system that:

- Establishes individual physiological baselines.
- Tracks changes over time.
- Identifies deviations from normal personal patterns.
- Detects persistent abnormal trends.
- Explains why an alert was generated.

### Possible Data Inputs

- Heart rate
- Blood pressure
- Oxygen saturation
- Body temperature
- Sleep duration
- Physical activity
- Exercise performance
- Medical history
- Self-reported symptoms

### Research Questions

- How should an astronaut's baseline be established?
- How should the baseline adapt to physiological changes during spaceflight?
- Which measurements are most useful for detecting early deterioration?
- How can the system distinguish normal adaptation from concerning changes?

## Gap 2: Multi-Signal Health Risk Correlation

### Problem

Spaceflight hazards can affect multiple human body systems simultaneously.

Traditional monitoring approaches may evaluate individual measurements independently, potentially overlooking meaningful combinations of changes.

For example, an increase in resting heart rate may not be concerning by itself. However, when combined with reduced sleep quality and declining exercise performance, it may warrant further assessment.

### Why It Matters

- Multiple physiological systems interact.
- Spaceflight hazards can produce combined effects.
- Individual measurements may not reveal the complete health situation.
- Interactions between environmental and physiological conditions are difficult to interpret.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a multi-signal health analysis engine that:

- Combines measurements from different sources.
- Identifies relationships between physiological changes.
- Tracks multiple indicators over time.
- Identifies clusters of potentially concerning changes.
- Explains which measurements contributed to an alert.

### Example

```text
Reduced sleep quality
        |
        v
Increased fatigue
        |
        v
Reduced exercise performance
        |
        v
Changes in physiological measurements
        |
        v
Combined health assessment
```

### Research Questions

- Which physiological measurements should be analyzed together?
- Which combinations of changes are clinically meaningful?
- How can we identify meaningful patterns without generating excessive false alarms?
- How can we distinguish correlation from causation?

## Gap 3: Autonomous Medical Decision Support During Deep-Space Missions

### Problem

Communication delays and limited access to medical specialists create significant challenges during deep-space missions.

Astronauts may need to assess medical situations without immediate assistance from Earth.

### Why It Matters

- Real-time medical consultation may not be possible.
- Medical supplies are limited.
- Diagnostic equipment may be unavailable.
- Emergency decisions may need to be made onboard.
- Communication interruptions may affect medical operations.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop an offline-first medical decision-support system that:

- Provides locally available medical reference information.
- Stores approved emergency procedures.
- Guides astronauts through structured assessment checklists.
- Organizes symptoms and medical events.
- Provides explainable next-step recommendations.
- Records decisions and actions for later medical review.

### Research Questions

- Which medical procedures can safely be supported autonomously?
- How can the system operate without internet connectivity?
- How should medical knowledge be updated?
- How can the system explain recommendations?
- How can it avoid providing unsafe or unsupported medical instructions?

**Important:** The system should support approved medical procedures and qualified medical judgment, not replace them.

## Gap 4: Cardiovascular Changes and Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)

### Problem

Microgravity changes fluid distribution and cardiovascular physiology.

Venous blood-flow changes can contribute to the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), which includes deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.

Blood clots can become life-threatening.

### Why It Matters

- Symptoms may be subtle or absent.
- Blood circulation changes during spaceflight.
- Early assessment can be difficult.
- Specialized clinical investigations may be required.
- Treatment and prevention must account for the spacecraft environment.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a cardiovascular risk-monitoring module that:

- Records relevant medical history and risk factors.
- Tracks available cardiovascular measurements.
- Collects reported symptoms.
- Identifies changes requiring medical review.
- Supports structured assessment and escalation.
- Stores relevant clinical examination results.

### Possible Data Inputs

- Heart rate
- Blood pressure
- ECG
- Relevant medical history
- Reported leg swelling or pain
- Relevant ultrasound findings, when available

### Research Questions

- Which measurements are useful for cardiovascular monitoring in microgravity?
- How can changes in blood circulation be assessed?
- What information is required for astronaut-specific VTE risk assessment?
- How can the system identify when further clinical investigation is necessary?

**Important:** Ordinary wearable sensors cannot reliably diagnose or rule out blood clots.

## Gap 5: Spaceflight-Associated Neuro-Ocular Syndrome (SANS)

### Problem

Some astronauts experience changes in eye structure and visual function during or after spaceflight.

These changes are associated with Spaceflight-Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS).

The precise mechanisms, individual susceptibility, and long-term consequences are not completely understood.

### Why It Matters

- Visual changes can affect mission performance.
- Some changes require specialized medical examinations.
- Ordinary vital signs cannot adequately assess ocular health.
- Individual susceptibility varies.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop an ocular health monitoring module that:

- Records visual-function assessments.
- Tracks reported visual symptoms.
- Stores retinal imaging results when available.
- Organizes ocular ultrasound findings.
- Tracks changes throughout the mission.
- Alerts the medical team when relevant changes require review.

### Research Questions

- Which measurements are most useful for tracking SANS?
- How do ocular changes develop during long-duration missions?
- Which astronauts may be more susceptible?
- How can visual changes be monitored over time?

## Gap 6: Sleep, Circadian Disruption, and Fatigue

### Problem

Mission schedules, lighting, noise, workload, and environmental conditions can disrupt astronauts' sleep and circadian rhythms.

Poor sleep can affect cognitive performance, reaction time, decision-making, and emotional regulation.

### Why It Matters

- Fatigue may accumulate over multiple days.
- Self-reported tiredness may not represent actual performance.
- Poor sleep can affect mission safety.
- Sleep disruption can interact with psychological stress.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a sleep and fatigue monitoring module that:

- Tracks sleep duration and quality.
- Monitors sleep patterns over time.
- Combines sleep data with workload and activity.
- Identifies persistent fatigue trends.
- Provides health summaries for astronauts and medical teams.

### Possible Data Inputs

- Sleep duration
- Sleep quality
- Activity levels
- Heart-rate patterns
- Workload
- Self-reported fatigue

### Research Questions

- How does microgravity affect sleep quality?
- Which indicators are useful for identifying fatigue?
- How can the system distinguish temporary tiredness from persistent deterioration?
- How does sleep disruption affect cognitive performance?

## Gap 7: Cognitive and Behavioral Health Monitoring

### Problem

Isolation, confinement, workload, interpersonal stress, and disrupted sleep can affect astronauts' psychological well-being and cognitive performance.

Some behavioral and cognitive changes are difficult to identify through conventional physiological monitoring.

### Why It Matters

- Normal vital signs do not guarantee normal cognitive performance.
- Isolation can affect psychological well-being.
- Cognitive deterioration can affect mission safety.
- Behavioral changes may develop gradually.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a cognitive and behavioral health monitoring module that:

- Provides voluntary mood assessments.
- Tracks self-reported stress and fatigue.
- Monitors sleep and workload trends.
- Includes periodic cognitive tests when approved.
- Identifies changes that may require further assessment.

### Possible Data Inputs

- Mood assessments
- Stress questionnaires
- Sleep data
- Workload
- Reaction-time tests
- Cognitive assessment results

### Research Questions

- Which behavioral indicators are useful for monitoring astronaut well-being?
- How can cognitive deterioration be detected?
- Which assessment methods are suitable for long-duration missions?
- How can sensitive psychological information be protected?

## Gap 8: Muscle Atrophy and Bone Loss

### Problem

Microgravity reduces the mechanical loading experienced by muscles and bones.

This can cause muscle deterioration and bone resorption.

Exercise countermeasures help but do not completely eliminate these problems.

### Why It Matters

- Bone density can decrease during spaceflight.
- Muscle strength can deteriorate.
- Long-duration missions increase the importance of maintaining physical capability.
- Changes may persist after returning to Earth.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a musculoskeletal health monitoring module that:

- Tracks exercise adherence.
- Records exercise performance.
- Monitors strength assessments.
- Stores bone-density measurements.
- Tracks relevant laboratory biomarkers.
- Visualizes longitudinal changes.

### Research Questions

- Which indicators best represent muscle deterioration?
- How can bone health be monitored during long-duration missions?
- How effective are current exercise countermeasures?
- How can software identify declining physical performance?

## Gap 9: Immune Dysfunction and Infection Monitoring

### Problem

Spaceflight can alter immune responses and microbial behavior.

Microorganisms can also change within closed spacecraft environments.

Infection is particularly important because treatment options are limited during missions.

### Why It Matters

- Immune responses may change.
- Latent viruses can reactivate.
- Microbial environments can change.
- Symptoms may initially be nonspecific.
- Medical treatment resources are limited.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop an infection and immune-health monitoring module that:

- Tracks symptoms and body temperature.
- Records relevant laboratory results.
- Organizes infection-related medical events.
- Tracks follow-up assessments.
- Connects individual health information with environmental monitoring data when appropriate.

### Research Questions

- How does spaceflight affect immune function?
- Which biomarkers are useful for monitoring immune changes?
- How can infection risks be identified early?
- How do spacecraft microbial environments affect astronaut health?

## Gap 10: Sensor Reliability and Data Quality

### Problem

Healthcare monitoring systems depend on accurate and reliable data.

Wearable sensors can generate noisy, incomplete, delayed, or incorrect measurements.

Movement and unusual operating conditions can further affect sensor performance.

### Why It Matters

- Sensor artifacts can generate false alarms.
- Missing measurements can hide meaningful changes.
- Different devices may use different units and sampling rates.
- Communication interruptions can delay data delivery.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a data-quality management layer that:

- Validates incoming measurements.
- Normalizes units.
- Checks timestamps.
- Detects missing data.
- Identifies implausible readings.
- Tracks sensor reliability.
- Displays data-quality indicators.
- Supports offline storage and synchronization.

### Research Questions

- How reliable are wearable sensors in microgravity?
- Which measurements can be collected continuously?
- How can sensor artifacts be identified?
- How should the system handle missing measurements?
- How can data from multiple devices be standardized?

---

## Gap 11: Longitudinal Health Records and Post-Mission Follow-Up

### Problem

Some health effects of spaceflight may persist or become apparent after astronauts return to Earth.

A single measurement cannot represent an astronaut's complete health trajectory.

### Why It Matters

- Pre-flight health information is important for comparison.
- Health changes develop over time.
- Post-flight recovery should be monitored.
- Long-term research requires consistent health records.

### Potential Software Solution

Develop a longitudinal astronaut health record system that:

- Stores pre-flight health information.
- Tracks in-flight measurements.
- Records medical events.
- Maintains post-flight health assessments.
- Compares health trends across different mission stages.
- Generates structured medical reports.

### Research Questions

- Which health changes persist after returning to Earth?
- How long should astronauts be monitored after a mission?
- Which measurements are most useful for longitudinal analysis?
- How can health records be standardized across missions?

# 4. Recommended MVP Scope

For a hackathon prototype, building every monitoring module is unnecessary.

A more practical approach is to develop one integrated system with five core capabilities.

## 4.1 Personalized Health Baseline

Establish an individual astronaut's normal health measurements.

Features:

- Individual health profile.
- Historical measurements.
- Baseline comparison.
- Trend visualization.

## 4.2 Multi-Signal Early Warning Engine

Identify potentially concerning changes across multiple indicators.

Features:

- Trend detection.
- Anomaly detection.
- Multi-signal correlation.
- Explainable alerts.
- Severity and uncertainty indicators.

## 4.3 Data Quality Management

Ensure that the system does not make decisions based on unreliable measurements.

Features:

- Missing-data detection.
- Sensor validation.
- Timestamp validation.
- Unit normalization.
- Data-quality indicators.

## 4.4 Offline Medical Decision Support

Provide astronauts with locally accessible medical information and structured assessment workflows.

Features:

- Offline medical references.
- Emergency procedures.
- Assessment checklists.
- Medical event recording.
- Escalation instructions.

Medical content must be approved by qualified experts.

## 4.5 Mission Health Dashboard

Provide an integrated overview of astronaut health.

Features:

- Current health status.
- Historical trends.
- Alerts and warnings.
- Health assessment summaries.
- Medical event history.
- Mission health reports.

# 7. Functional Requirements

The healthcare monitoring system should:

1. Ingest measurements from multiple sources.
2. Normalize measurement units and timestamps.
3. Associate each measurement with an astronaut and source device.
4. Maintain individual health baselines.
5. Track historical health measurements.
6. Detect abnormal trends.
7. Identify multi-signal patterns.
8. Explain why an alert was generated.
9. Display measurement quality and uncertainty.
10. Support offline operation.
11. Synchronize data after network reconnection.
12. Record medical assessments and actions.
13. Generate structured health summaries.
14. Provide role-based access to sensitive health information.

# 8. Safety and Reliability Requirements

Healthcare software for astronauts must prioritize reliability and safety.

Important requirements:

- The software must not claim to replace a qualified medical professional.
- Alerts must explain the evidence behind them.
- Missing measurements must never be interpreted as normal measurements.
- Risk scores must not be presented as confirmed diagnoses.
- Medical recommendations must come from approved sources.
- Sensitive health information must be protected.
- Critical functionality must remain usable during network outages.
- The system must communicate uncertainty.
- False alarms and missed alerts must be evaluated.
- All important medical events and actions should be auditable.

# 11. Recommended Research Workflow

1. **Select a mission scenario:** ISS, lunar mission, or Mars/deep-space mission.
2. **Select a primary research problem:** Personalized early warning, VTE monitoring, or autonomous medical decision support.
3. **Review NASA risk documentation:** Identify relevant hazards, research tasks, countermeasures, and remaining gaps.
4. **Find suitable datasets:** Examine available variables, populations, limitations, and mission contexts.
5. **Define system requirements:** Specify inputs, outputs, latency, offline functionality, and safety constraints.
6. **Build a transparent baseline:** Begin with rules and trend analysis before introducing machine learning.
7. **Evaluate realistically:** Test normal variation, noisy sensors, missing data, false alarms, and simulated emergencies.
8. **Document limitations:** Separate demonstrated capabilities from future research possibilities.

### Research Verification Guidelines

For each selected research gap:

- Identify the relevant NASA risk.
- Read the underlying research task.
- Find recent scientific publications.
- Identify existing countermeasures.
- Determine what remains uncertain.
- Verify whether relevant datasets are available.
- Avoid describing an existing research challenge as completely unsolved without evidence.

A research gap in a roadmap does not automatically mean NASA has no existing mitigation or monitoring capability.

# 13. Suggested Project Problem Statement

> Astronauts experience complex physiological and behavioral changes during spaceflight, while limited onboard medical resources and communication delays can make timely medical assessment difficult.
>
> Existing measurements may be fragmented across different devices, assessments, and medical records.
>
> We propose an astronaut healthcare monitoring system that integrates relevant health data, establishes personalized baselines, identifies meaningful changes across multiple indicators, and provides explainable, offline-capable medical decision support.
>
> The system is designed to assist astronauts and medical teams rather than replace clinical judgment. It aims to improve early awareness, health-data interpretation, and medical decision-making during long-duration space missions.

# 14. Final Recommendation

The strongest product direction for this project is:

**An intelligent astronaut healthcare monitoring system that detects early physiological changes, correlates multiple health indicators, and supports autonomous medical decision-making during deep-space missions.**

The main differentiation should not simply be the use of AI.

It should be the ability to:

1. Detect meaningful changes before they become serious.
2. Understand individual astronaut health patterns.
3. Correlate multiple physiological indicators.
4. Explain why an alert was generated.
5. Work without continuous Earth connectivity.
6. Support evidence-based medical decisions.
7. Communicate uncertainty and data limitations.

**Core product principle:**

> Detect changes. Explain evidence. Support decisions. Communicate limitations.

This is a more defensible and meaningful direction than building another dashboard that only displays vital signs.
