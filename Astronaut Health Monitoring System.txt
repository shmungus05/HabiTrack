# Astronaut Health Monitoring System
## Research Notes: SpaceX Inspiration4 — Molecular and Physiological Changes in the Civilian Crew

**Research source:** Jones et al. (2024), *Nature*  
**Published:** June 11, 2024  
**Mission:** SpaceX Inspiration4  
**Mission duration:** Approximately 3 days  
**Crew:** 4 civilian astronauts

**Original research:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07648-x

---

# 1. Executive Summary

This research investigates how human physiology, cognition, immune responses and other biological systems change during spaceflight.

The researchers studied four civilian astronauts before, during and after their three-day orbital mission.

The study used multiple monitoring technologies:

- Smartwatch-based physiological monitoring.
- Cardiovascular measurements.
- Cognitive performance assessments.
- Sleep and mood surveys.
- Ocular alignment measurements.
- Handheld ultrasound imaging.
- Blood and saliva sampling.
- Molecular and immune profiling.
- Spacecraft environmental monitoring.

### Most important finding

Astronaut health cannot be understood through vital signs alone.

A reliable astronaut health monitoring system should combine physiological, cognitive, behavioural and environmental data while tracking changes against each astronaut's personal baseline.

**Implication for our project:** We should develop a multimodal astronaut health monitoring and early-warning system rather than simply creating a dashboard displaying heart rate and oxygen levels.

# 2. Important Scientific Findings

## 2.1 Cardiovascular Monitoring

### What the researchers monitored

The crew used Apple Watch Series 6 devices to collect:

- Heart Rate (HR).
- Heart Rate Variability (HRV).
- Blood Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂).
- Physical activity.
- Active energy expenditure.

### What they discovered

The researchers observed significant changes in heart rate and HRV across mission phases.

However, individual astronauts responded differently.

One astronaut experienced decreased heart rate and increased HRV during flight, accompanied by lower blood oxygen saturation.

The crew also exhibited substantially lower physical activity and active energy expenditure during flight compared with pre-flight measurements.

Most cardiovascular measurements returned to baseline after the mission.

### Why this matters

1. Microgravity can influence cardiovascular physiology.
2. Astronauts do not necessarily respond to spaceflight in identical ways.
3. Monitoring multiple physiological signals is more informative than monitoring one measurement.
4. Physical activity provides useful context for interpreting physiological changes.

### Application to our system

Implement a cardiovascular monitoring module.

| Parameter | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Heart Rate | Monitor cardiovascular changes |
| HRV | Track changes in autonomic regulation |
| SpO₂ | Monitor estimated blood oxygen saturation |
| Activity | Understand physical activity patterns |
| Energy Expenditure | Monitor changes in energy consumption |

**Recommended visualization:** Time-series charts comparing current readings with individual pre-flight baselines.

## 2.2 Cognitive Performance and Mental Health

### What the researchers monitored

The researchers used a cognitive test battery containing ten different assessments.

These covered:

- Attention.
- Reaction speed.
- Memory.
- Reasoning.
- Emotion recognition.
- Motor performance.
- Cognitive processing.

They also collected mood and alertness surveys.

### What they discovered

The study identified reduced cognitive efficiency during spaceflight.

Some cognitive effects were particularly influenced by one astronaut.

The astronauts reported:

- Average sleep duration of approximately 6.7 ± 0.7 hours per night.
- Moderate stress.
- High workload.

Post-flight cognitive performance generally returned to pre-flight levels, although an exception was reported for one cognitive task.

### Why this matters

An astronaut might have normal-looking physiological measurements while experiencing reduced attention or slower decision-making.

This can affect:

- Emergency response.
- Mission-critical decisions.
- Equipment operation.
- Communication.
- Complex task execution.

### Application to our system

Create a cognitive and psychological monitoring module.

Monitor:

| Parameter | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Sleep Duration | Understand sleep patterns |
| Sleep Quality | Track perceived recovery |
| Alertness | Evaluate subjective readiness |
| Stress Level | Monitor psychological stress |
| Mood | Track emotional changes |
| Reaction Time | Identify changes in response performance |
| Cognitive Performance | Track performance over time |

**Recommended feature:** Short cognitive assessments that astronauts can complete periodically.

## 2.3 Vestibular System and Space Motion Sickness

### What the researchers investigated

The study examined ocular misalignment to understand changes associated with the neurovestibular system.

The vestibular system helps humans understand:

- Balance.
- Movement.
- Spatial orientation.
- Head position.
- Acceleration.

The otolith organs in the inner ear are particularly important for sensing gravity and linear acceleration.

### What they discovered

Two out of four astronauts experienced Space Motion Sickness (SMS).

The researchers observed differences in ocular alignment patterns between astronauts who experienced motion sickness and those who did not.

Microgravity changes how the brain processes sensory information related to orientation and movement.

### Why this matters

Space motion sickness can affect an astronaut's ability to:

- Maintain orientation.
- Perform physical activities.
- Concentrate.
- Operate equipment.
- Complete mission tasks.

### Application to our system

Include a vestibular and symptom-monitoring module.

Track:

- Nausea.
- Dizziness.
- Disorientation.
- Motion sensitivity.
- Balance-related difficulties.

An advanced version could incorporate guided vestibular assessments, provided they use validated procedures.

## 2.4 Handheld Ultrasound and Remote Medical Monitoring

### What the researchers used

The Inspiration4 crew used the Butterfly iQ+ handheld ultrasound device.

The astronauts performed their own ultrasound scans in space.

The researchers examined:

- Internal Jugular Vein (IJV).
- Urinary bladder.
- Ophthalmic structures.

They collected 108 in-flight imaging instances.

### Important observation

The research demonstrated that astronauts could collect certain medical images independently using portable equipment and just-in-time instructions.

However, image quality varied significantly depending on the anatomical target.

The ocular imaging data were particularly difficult to collect reliably.

### Why this matters

During long-duration space missions, astronauts cannot always depend on immediate assistance from medical specialists on Earth.

A future health monitoring platform should support autonomous medical data collection.

### Application to our system

Potential future features:

- Guided self-examination workflows.
- Ultrasound data integration.
- Medical image storage.
- Image quality assessment.
- Remote medical review.
- Procedure instructions.

This should be a future extension rather than an essential MVP feature.

## 2.5 Immune System and Molecular Changes

### What the researchers investigated

The study used multi-omics approaches to investigate biological changes.

Samples included:

- Blood.
- Saliva.
- Skin swabs.
- Skin biopsies.
- Environmental samples.

The researchers investigated DNA, RNA, proteins, metabolites, immune responses and microbiome data.

### Important observations

The research identified:

- Telomere elongation in all four crew members.
- Changes in gene-regulatory and immune-related signals.
- A spike in viral abundance during flight.
- Changes in certain molecular and inflammatory pathways.

These findings demonstrate that spaceflight can produce biological changes that cannot necessarily be detected through conventional wearable sensors.

### Why this matters

A comprehensive astronaut health platform should eventually support laboratory measurements alongside real-time physiological data.

### Application to our system

Consider a future laboratory integration module.

It could support:

- Blood test results.
- Immune biomarkers.
- Inflammatory markers.
- Molecular measurements.
- Historical laboratory comparisons.

Do not attempt to recreate laboratory multi-omics analysis in the initial hackathon prototype.

## 2.6 Spacecraft Environmental Monitoring

### What the researchers monitored

The researchers also examined environmental conditions inside the Dragon capsule.

Parameters included:

- Carbon dioxide (CO₂).
- Oxygen partial pressure.
- Temperature.
- Relative humidity.
- Cabin pressure.
- Sound pressure levels.

### Why this matters

An astronaut's health should not be analyzed independently of their surrounding environment.

Environmental conditions can provide important context for interpreting physiological and behavioural changes.

For example, the paper explored the relationship between cabin CO₂ levels and HRV.

However, correlation does not establish causation.

### Application to our system

Create an environmental monitoring module.

| Parameter | Purpose |
|---|---|
| CO₂ | Monitor cabin air conditions |
| O₂ | Monitor oxygen environment |
| Temperature | Monitor thermal conditions |
| Humidity | Monitor moisture conditions |
| Pressure | Monitor cabin pressure |
| Sound | Monitor acoustic environment |

Use validated spacecraft-specific operational limits rather than treating the Inspiration4 measurements as universal safety thresholds.

# 3. Critical Engineering Lessons

## Lesson 1: Personal Baselines Are Essential

Every astronaut has different physiological characteristics.

A reading that is normal for one astronaut might represent a meaningful change for another.

**System requirement:**

Create individual pre-flight health profiles and compare new measurements against each astronaut's historical baseline.

## Lesson 2: Monitor Across Mission Phases

The research collected measurements during:

- Pre-flight.
- In-flight.
- Post-flight.

This helps identify temporary adaptations and longer-lasting changes.

**System requirement:**

Every health record should include:

- Astronaut ID.
- Measurement type.
- Value.
- Unit.
- Timestamp.
- Mission phase.
- Data source.
- Data quality.

## Lesson 3: Combine Multiple Data Sources

Physiological data alone cannot provide a complete picture.

**System requirement:**

Combine wearable data, environmental data, cognitive assessments and medical records.

## Lesson 4: Include Cognitive Readiness

Astronauts need to maintain attention, decision-making ability and operational performance.

**System requirement:**

Provide periodic cognitive assessments and psychological check-ins.

## Lesson 5: Data Quality Is Critical

The ultrasound experiments demonstrated that collecting data does not guarantee that the data are usable.

**System requirement:**

Track missing, invalid, outdated and low-quality measurements.

Never interpret missing data as normal health data.

## Lesson 6: Design for Autonomous Operation

Astronauts may need to perform health assessments without direct medical assistance.

**System requirement:**

Provide simple instructions, guided workflows and offline data collection.

## Lesson 7: Support Long-Term Data Management

The researchers emphasized the importance of standardized biomedical databases.

**System requirement:**

Build structured, interoperable health records that can be exported and analyzed across mission phases.

# 5. Recommended Early-Warning System

A useful system should not simply generate an alert whenever a measurement changes.

Instead, use a layered approach.

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | Normal update or minor trend | New HR measurement |
| Watch | Unusual or sustained deviation | HRV differs from personal baseline |
| Critical | Validated mission-specific safety rule triggered | Approved threshold exceeded |

Every alert should explain:

1. What changed?
2. When did it change?
3. Which measurements support the alert?
4. How does it compare with the astronaut's baseline?
5. Is the data reliable?
6. What approved procedure should be followed?

**Important:** The system must not claim to diagnose diseases. Use validated medical or mission-specific thresholds only when authoritative values are available.

# 6. Proposed High-Level Architecture

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    A[Wearable Sensors] --> D[Data Ingestion]
    B[Cognitive Tests and Check-ins] --> D
    C[Environmental Sensors] --> D
    E[Medical and Laboratory Data] --> D

    D --> F[Data Validation]
    F --> G[Health Data Storage]

    G --> H[Personal Baseline Engine]
    G --> I[Trend Analysis]

    H --> J[Explainable Alert Engine]
    I --> J

    J --> K[Astronaut Dashboard]
    J --> L[Medical Operator Review]

    G --> M[Research Data Export]
```

### Main Components

**Data Ingestion Layer**

Collect information from wearables, environmental sensors, medical records and manual assessments.

**Data Validation Layer**

Check units, timestamps, missing values and measurement quality.

**Health Data Storage**

Store normalized health records and preserve their original sources.

**Personal Baseline Engine**

Calculate individual reference values using pre-flight measurements.

**Trend Analysis Engine**

Identify unusual changes and sustained deviations.

**Explainable Alert Engine**

Generate alerts with supporting measurements and reasoning.

**Dashboard**

Display health trends, mission status, alerts and historical data.

# 7. Important Limitations of the Research

The Inspiration4 study is valuable, but it has limitations.

1. Only four astronauts participated.
2. The mission lasted approximately three days.
3. Individual physiological responses varied.
4. Wearable monitoring was limited to a portion of the mission.
5. Some findings are exploratory and preliminary.
6. Specialized laboratory measurements cannot be reproduced using ordinary wearable sensors.

The researchers explicitly acknowledge that the small sample size and operational constraints limit the conclusions that can be drawn.

**How we should use this research:**

Use it to understand what should be monitored and how data should be organized. Do not treat its observations as universal medical thresholds.

# 10. Final Recommendations for Our Hackathon Team

If we remember only five things from this research:

1. **Personalized monitoring:** Every astronaut needs an individual baseline.
2. **Multimodal monitoring:** Combine physiological, cognitive, behavioural and environmental information.
3. **Longitudinal analysis:** Track changes over time rather than relying on isolated readings.
4. **Explainable alerts:** Show why the system identifies a potential problem.
5. **Realistic implementation:** Focus on reliable monitoring and early warnings before implementing complex medical diagnostics.

## Recommended Product Direction

Build an:

**Astronaut Health Monitoring and Early-Warning System**

The system should collect available health and environmental data, compare measurements against individual baselines, identify unusual patterns and provide understandable alerts to support astronaut and mission-operator decisions.

The goal is not simply to monitor astronauts.

The goal is to help identify potentially concerning changes early enough for appropriate human review and intervention.
